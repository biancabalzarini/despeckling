\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) is a highly effective remote sensing technology capable of generating high-resolution
images of the Earth's surface regardless of weather or lighting conditions. This makes SAR imagery an invaluable
tool for applications ranging from environmental monitoring to terrain change detection and disaster management.
However, a significant drawback of SAR images is the inherent presence of speckle noise. Unlike the additive noise
common in optical imagery, speckle is a multiplicative, non-Gaussian phenomenon resulting from the coherent
interference of radar waves scattered by surface elements. This granular noise severely degrades the visual quality
of SAR images and complicates subsequent analysis and interpretation tasks.

Over the years, various techniques have been proposed for speckle mitigation. Classical approaches include spatial
filters such as the Lee \parencite{lee1980digital} and Frost \parencite{frost1982model} filters, as well as Maximum a
Posteriori (MAP) estimators \parencite{kuan1987adaptive}. Transform-domain methods, such as those based on wavelets
\parencite{zhang2017nonmodel} or Shearlets \parencite{hou2012shearlet}, have also been widely utilized. While advanced
algorithms like Block-Matching and 3D filtering (BM3D) \parencite{parrilli2012nonlocal} are often considered the state of
the art among classical methods, they still struggle to balance effective noise reduction with the preservation of
fine structural details, frequently introducing blurring or incurring high computational costs.

Recently, deep learning techniques---particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Autoencoders---have
revolutionized image denoising \parencite{zhang2018dilated,vitale2021multiobjective,lattari2019deep,cardona2025optimization}.
These models typically operate within a supervised learning framework, requiring large datasets of paired noisy and
clean images to learn an effective mapping. Since acquiring real, perfectly noise-free SAR images is practically
impossible, researchers must rely on synthetic data generation. This involves artificially injecting simulated
speckle noise into clean optical or simulated images to create the necessary training pairs. Alternatively, large
series of temporal images can be averaged to approximate a clean ground truth \parencite{vasquez2024labeled}, but this
data is scarce and hard to process.

The efficacy of supervised deep learning models is heavily dependent on the realism of the simulated speckle noise.
Existing literature often employs relatively simple statistical models for noise generation, such as the Gamma or
Rayleigh distributions. While computationally convenient, these models are limited; for instance, the Rayleigh
distribution is only statistically accurate for specific homogeneous areas like pasturelands \parencite{lattari2019deep}.
They fail to adequately capture the complex statistical properties of actual speckle across heterogeneous terrains,
such as urban environments or dense vegetation, thereby limiting the generalization capability of the trained
networks.

To address this gap, this paper proposes the use of the \( \mathcal{G}_I^0 \) distribution for simulating speckle
noise. The \( \mathcal{G}_I^0 \) distribution is renowned for its flexibility and robustness, accurately modeling
speckle across a wide variety of surface roughness levels and heterogeneous clutter \parencite{originalGI0}. Despite its
proven accuracy in the statistical modeling of SAR data, its integration into the synthetic data generation pipeline
for training deep learning despeckling models remains relatively unexplored. This work investigates how
incorporating the \( \mathcal{G}_I^0 \) distribution for dataset preparation can enhance the training quality,
leading to more robust and generalizable convolutional autoencoder models for SAR image despeckling.
