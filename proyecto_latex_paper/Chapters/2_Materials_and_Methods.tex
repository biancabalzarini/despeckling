\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{SAR Images}

For this study, two types of datasets were used: synthetically generated images and actual SAR images. The synthetic dataset was created based on the $\mathcal{G}_I^0$ distribution, which models the statistical properties of speckle noise. 
A random variable $z$ following the $\mathcal{G}_I^0$ distribution can be expressed as the product of two independent random variables:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:zxy}
z = x \cdot y
\end{equation}
where $x$ represents the noise-free backscatter, modeled by an inverse Gamma distribution ($x \sim \Gamma^{-1}(-\alpha, 1/\gamma)$), and $y$ represents the speckle noise, modeled by a Gamma distribution ($y \sim \Gamma(L, 1/L)$). The parameter $\alpha$ is related to the texture or roughness of the surface, while $L$ is the number of looks of the SAR system. For instance, $\alpha$ values close to zero (e.g., -1 to -3) correspond to heterogeneous urban areas, while more negative values (e.g., -10 to -20) represent homogeneous surfaces like calm water. This model allowed us to generate pairs of clean ($x$) and noisy ($z$) images for training the proposed models. To create a robust training dataset, we generated synthetic images structured in quadrants, where each quadrant simulated a different surface texture by assigning a randomly chosen value for the $\alpha$ parameter, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:synthetic_quadrants_examples}. This approach, by randomizing the textures in each image, aims to prevent the model from simply memorizing a static pattern of textures. Instead, the goal is to force the model to learn how to filter noise in the presence of heterogeneous textures, thus promoting better generalization. Additionally, by mixing images with different partitioning schemes during training (e.g., some images divided into 4 quadrants, others into 9 or more), the model is further prevented from learning fixed spatial boundaries, as the location and number of region edges varies across samples.

A strategy to improve generalization was training certain models (such as A20) on a mixed dataset combining synthetic images with different partition granularities. This mixed data approach ensures that the model encounters greater structural diversity during training, preventing overfitting to a particular spatial structure and promoting robustness when applied to real SAR data with varying textures and boundaries.

A key concept in SAR image analysis is the 'number of looks' (L), which refers to the number of independent images of the same scene that are averaged to reduce speckle noise. A higher number of looks results in a smoother image with less noise. The 'Equivalent Number of Looks' (ENL) is a widely used metric to estimate the effectiveness of a speckle filter. It quantifies the smoothness of a homogeneous area in the filtered image. A higher ENL value indicates a greater reduction in noise and, therefore, a better filter performance.

The actual SAR images used in this work are from the dataset provided by \cite{vasquez2024labeled}, which contains pairs of noisy and clean images obtained through temporal averaging. An example of such a pair is shown in Figure \ref{fig:sar_images}. These images present varied textures, including urban areas, vegetation, and water bodies, which are suitable for training and testing the generalization capability of the despeckling filters.

\subsection{Methods}

\subsubsection{Autoencoder Architecture}
The core of our despeckling method is a convolutional autoencoder. An autoencoder is a neural network designed to learn a compressed representation (encoding) of its input and then reconstruct it (decoding). The network is composed of an encoder, which maps the input image to a lower-dimensional latent space, and a decoder, which reconstructs the image from the latent representation.

Our implementation uses a convolutional architecture. The encoder consists of a series of convolutional layers that apply filters to the input image, extracting hierarchical features and reducing spatial dimensions. The decoder uses transposed convolutional layers (also known as deconvolutions) to upsample the feature maps and reconstruct the image. The general structure of the autoencoder is symmetrical. Figure \ref{fig:esquema_red} shows a scheme of a particular architecture. A key aspect of our training process is that the network takes a noisy image as input and is trained to reconstruct the corresponding clean (noise-free) version, which is provided as the target output. The model is trained by minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss between the reconstructed image and the clean target image. Because the training is supervised with a known clean target, MSE is a natural and stable choice for the reconstruction objective.

The statistical figures of merit based on the ratio image and the GLCM are used as complementary evaluation criteria rather than direct loss terms, since they are not differentiable and cannot be straightforwardly optimized by gradient descent.

    \subsubsection{Evaluation Metrics}
To quantitatively assess the performance of the despeckling filter, we used statistical metrics based on the multiplicative nature of speckle noise. Assuming the noisy image $z$ is the product of the clean image $x$ and the speckle noise $y$ ($z = x \cdot y$), the ratio image $I = z / \hat{x}$ (where $\hat{x}$ is the filtered image) should ideally contain only speckle noise with no remaining structural information. Based on this principle, we used two metrics proposed by \cite{gomez2017unassisted}.

The first metric, focused on first-order statistics ($r_{\textnormal{ENL},\mu}$), evaluates the preservation of the mean and the level of smoothness in homogeneous regions. It is based on two conditions: the mean of the ratio image $\mu_I$ should be close to 1, and the filter should increase smoothness. Smoothness is measured by the Equivalent Number of Looks ($\text{ENL} = (\mu/\sigma)^2$), where $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the mean and standard deviation of a homogeneous area. The metric combines these two aspects for $N$ selected homogeneous regions:
\begin{equation}
    r_{\text{ENL},\mu} = \frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( \frac{\left|\text{ENL}_z(i) - \text{ENL}_I(i)\right|}{\text{ENL}_z(i)} + \left|1-\mu_I(i)\right| \right).
\end{equation}
A low $r_{\text{ENL},\mu}$ indicates that the filter successfully removes noise while preserving the average brightness.

The second metric, for second-order statistics ($\delta_h$), quantifies the amount of residual geometric structure in the ratio image by testing its texture properties' invariance to random permutations. An ideal ratio image, being pure noise, should have texture properties that do not change significantly when its pixels are randomly shuffled. This is measured using the homogeneity descriptor ($h$) from the Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM). The metric $\delta_h$ calculates the percentage variation between the homogeneity of the original ratio image ($h_0$) and the average homogeneity of several randomly permuted versions of it ($\bar{h}_m$):
\begin{equation}
    \delta_h = 100 \cdot \left| 1 - \frac{\bar{h}_m}{h_0} \right|.
\end{equation}
A value close to zero implies that no significant geometric information remains in the ratio image, indicating a successful filtering.

Finally, we combine these two metrics into a single figure of merit, $\mathcal{M} = r_{\textnormal{ENL},\mu} + \delta_h$, where a lower value indicates better despeckling performance.
