\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{SAR Images}

For this study, two types of datasets were used: synthetically generated images and real SAR images. The synthetic dataset was created based on the $\mathcal{G}_I^0$ distribution, which models the statistical properties of speckle noise. 
A random variable $z$ following the $\mathcal{G}_I^0$ distribution can be expressed as the product of two independent random variables:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:zxy}
z = x \cdot y
\end{equation}
where $x$ represents the noise-free backscatter, modeled by an inverse Gamma distribution ($x \sim \Gamma^{-1}(-\alpha, 1/\gamma)$), and $y$ represents the speckle noise, modeled by a Gamma distribution ($y \sim \Gamma(L, 1/L)$). The parameter $\alpha$ is related to the texture or roughness of the surface, while $L$ is the number of looks of the SAR system. This model allowed us to generate pairs of clean ($x$) and noisy ($z$) images for training the proposed models.

A key concept in SAR image analysis is the 'number of looks' (L), which refers to the number of independent images of the same scene that are averaged to reduce speckle noise. A higher number of looks results in a smoother image with less noise. The 'Equivalent Number of Looks' (ENL) is a widely used metric to estimate the effectiveness of a speckle filter. It quantifies the smoothness of a homogeneous area in the filtered image. A higher ENL value indicates a greater reduction in noise and, therefore, a better filter performance.

The real SAR images used in this work are from the dataset provided by \cite{vasquez2024labeled}, which contains pairs of noisy and clean images obtained through temporal averaging. An example of such a pair is shown in Figure \ref{fig:sar_images}. These images present varied textures, including urban areas, vegetation, and water bodies, which are suitable for training and testing the generalization capability of the despeckling filters.

\subsection{Methods}

\subsubsection{Autoencoder Architecture}
The core of our despeckling method is a convolutional autoencoder. An autoencoder is a neural network designed to learn a compressed representation (encoding) of its input and then reconstruct it (decoding). The network is composed of an encoder, which maps the input image to a lower-dimensional latent space, and a decoder, which reconstructs the image from the latent representation.

Our implementation uses a convolutional architecture. The encoder consists of a series of convolutional layers that apply filters to the input image, extracting hierarchical features and reducing spatial dimensions. The decoder uses transposed convolutional layers (also known as deconvolutions) to upsample the feature maps and reconstruct the image. The general structure of the autoencoder is symmetrical. Figure \ref{fig:esquema_red} shows a schematic of the architecture. A key aspect of our training process is that the network takes a noisy image as input and is trained to reconstruct the corresponding clean (noise-free) version, which is provided as the target output. The model is trained by minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss between the reconstructed image and the clean target image.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Metrics}
To quantitatively assess the performance of the despeckling filter, we used statistical metrics based on the multiplicative nature of speckle noise. Assuming the noisy image $z$ is the product of the clean image $x$ and the speckle noise $y$ ($z = x \cdot y$), the ratio image $I = z / \hat{x}$ (where $\hat{x}$ is the filtered image) should ideally contain only speckle noise with no remaining structural information.

Based on this principle, we used two metrics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First-order statistics ($r_{\textnormal{ENL},\mu}$):} This metric evaluates the preservation of the mean and the increase in the Equivalent Number of Looks (ENL) in homogeneous areas of the image. A good filter should preserve the mean brightness while smoothing the noise, thus increasing the ENL.
    \item \textbf{Second-order statistics ($\delta_h$):} This metric measures the amount of residual geometric information in the ratio image $I$ by analyzing the homogeneity descriptor ($h$) derived from the Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM). For a perfect filter, the ratio image should have no structure, and its statistical properties should be invariant to random pixel permutations. $\delta_h$ quantifies the change in homogeneity after such permutations.
\end{itemize}

Finally, we combine these two metrics into a single figure of merit, $\mathcal{M} = r_{\textnormal{ENL},\mu} + \delta_h$, where a lower value indicates better despeckling performance.
